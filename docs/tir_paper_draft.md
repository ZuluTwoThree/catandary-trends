# Do Patent-Based Technological Improvement Rate Predictions Travel? An Independent Replication on a Worldwide Citation Graph, with a Gated Dynamic Extension

**[Author], Catandary — independent research; draft v2, 2026-07-25**

---

## Abstract

Published patent-based forecasts of technological improvement rates (TIRs) for 1,757 technology domains (Singh et al., 2021) rest on a single implementation and a single national patent corpus. We reimplement the underlying SPNP-centrality method independently on a worldwide DOCDB citation graph (42.58 million nodes, 145.5 million citation edges), with deliberately different implementation choices — 100 instead of 1,000 network randomizations (shown sufficient by a direct R-ablation), CPC-based null-model constraints, an age-capped cited-centrality predictor — and recalibrate against the original 29 ground-truth domains (R² = 0.574, leave-one-out R² = 0.516). Benchmarked against the published forecasts at native domain granularity, our estimates reach Spearman 0.679 (95% cluster-bootstrap CI [0.623, 0.726]) and Pearson 0.798 (ln K; CI [0.762, 0.828]) over 651 well-populated domains (n ≥ 500 matched patents; 0.675 / 0.772 over 1,299 domains at n ≥ 100), with closely matching medians at the higher floor (11.0 vs. 11.02%/yr; 10.0 vs. 10.97%/yr at n ≥ 100) — substantial but incomplete agreement, with a measurable gap left by corpus and implementation variance. (The reference method's MCCV test correlation of ≈0.72 is a predicted-versus-observed statistic under a different validation design and is no ceiling for cross-implementation agreement.) We further quantify a corpus effect (worldwide normalization depresses levels and rank agreement relative to a US-restricted population), a pronounced invariance of the domain-mean predictor to set-membership noise (set F1 median 0.496, predictor correlation 0.987), and a systematic compression of the fastest domains that the fitted linear crosswalk cannot repair; errors-in-variables and quantile–quantile analyses confirm the compression as a genuine measurement property of our substrate (domain-level Deming slope ≈1, but an upper tail ~24% shorter). Rankings are robust to corpus and implementation variance; the absolute scale is substrate-bound. A rolling K(t) extension is deployed behind explicit abstention gates; a preregistered out-of-sample direction holdout scores 4/10, validating the false-positive control (4/4 mundane domains, zero polarity errors) but not directional sensitivity.

## 1 Introduction

Deciding where to direct R&D budgets, procurement, and policy attention requires an answer to a deceptively simple question: *how fast is a given technology getting better?* A large body of empirical work has established that functional performance in most technological domains improves exponentially over time, so that each domain can be characterized by a single yearly technological improvement rate (TIR) *K* — a generalized Moore's law (Moore, 1965; Magee et al., 2016). These rates differ enormously between domains — the most comprehensive published estimates span 2%/yr to 216%/yr (Singh et al., 2021) — and it is the *relative* rate, more than the current performance level, that determines which of two competing technologies eventually prevails (Triulzi et al., 2020). Measuring *K* directly, however, requires curated performance time series, which are scarce, laborious to construct, and available for only a few dozen domains (Magee et al., 2016). Patent data, by contrast, cover essentially the entire technological landscape over more than a century, are objectively delimited, and carry rich citation structure. The research line developed at MIT by Benson, Magee, Triulzi, Alstott, and Singh (Benson and Magee, 2015; Magee et al., 2016; Triulzi et al., 2020; Singh et al., 2021) exploits this: a normalized search path node pair (SPNP) centrality of a domain's patents in the citation network predicts the empirically measured improvement rate, culminating in published rate predictions for 1,757 technology domains covering 97.2% of the US patent system (Singh et al., 2021).

Despite the method's growing practical uptake, two gaps remain. First, to our knowledge there has been **no independent replication on a different patent corpus**: both prediction studies (Triulzi et al., 2020; Singh et al., 2021) operate on the same corpus of US utility grants (PatentsView, 1976–2015) and the same normalization pipeline, and all four studies in the line originate from one research group. Whether the predictor survives transplantation to a different data substrate — different patent office coverage, different citation-recording conventions, different classification systems (CPC rather than USPC), and a global rather than US-only network — is an open empirical question, and one with direct consequences for anyone deploying the method outside the original setting. Second, the published predictions are **static**: one time-constant *K* per domain, justified by the generalized-Moore's-law assumption, with an explicit warning that short-term rates are not reliably estimated (Triulzi et al., 2020). Production use, where users ask whether a technology is currently accelerating or maturing, requires a temporal extension — and guardrails that keep such an extension honest.

This paper reports an independent replication and extension of the patent-centrality TIR method on a **worldwide DOCDB citation graph** of 42.58 million patent nodes and 145.5 million citation edges spanning 1784–2026 — a corpus assembled entirely independently of PatentsView, containing 2.975 million of the 5.26 million US grants in the MIT patent-level reference data for 1976–2015 (≈57%; Section 3.1 describes the match procedure and the audited loss accounting). Because centrality percentiles are relative to their normalization population, coefficients cannot be transported between corpora; we therefore recalibrate the rate equation against the *exact* MIT calibration patent sets and performance-derived ground-truth rates (511,506 (domain, patent) rows; effectively n = 29 domains), obtaining, for the cited-centrality predictor, R² = 0.574, Spearman ρ = 0.764, and leave-one-out R² = 0.516 (own-centrality predictor: 0.521 / 0.712 / 0.459), with the active calibration K̂ = exp(−5.5622 + 5.5036·X̄)·exp(0.4930/2). Our implementation deliberately accepts several production-motivated approximations — R = 100 network randomizations instead of 1,000, CPC 3-character class preservation in the null model instead of main-USPC preservation, an age-3-capped endpoint of the cited-patent percentile instead of a t−1 snapshot, and publication year as grant-year proxy — and we quantify their cost. The full SPNP build with R = 100 completes in 114 minutes on a single CPU core (i9-11900K, 64 GB RAM, constant ~10–15 GB resident, no GPU); R = 1,000 would take an estimated 19–22 single-core hours for a noise reduction of only √R.

Our contributions are:

1. **Independent replication on a worldwide patent graph.** We re-implement the SPNP-centrality pipeline (identical centrality definition, t+3 measurement window, degree-/age-/class-preserving randomization, z-score, per-year rank percentile) on the 42.58M-node DOCDB world graph and recalibrate it on the exact MIT calibration sets (LOO-R² = 0.516, n = 29).
2. **A domain-level benchmark against all 1,757 published predictions.** We reconstruct the published classification-overlap-method (COM) domains from patent-level main-class × IPC4 assignments (1,751/1,757 domains populated; ln-size Pearson 0.819 against published domain sizes; median size ratio 0.724) and compare predicted rates domain by domain: at a floor of n ≥ 500 matched patents, 651 domains yield Spearman 0.679 and Pearson (ln K) 0.798, with nearly identical medians (11.0%/yr published vs. 11.02%/yr ours); at n ≥ 100, 1,299 domains yield 0.675 / 0.772. The comparison also isolates a real corpus effect: restricting our graph to US grants reproduces the published ranking at Spearman 0.704 over 526 IPC4 subclasses, whereas the full world corpus yields 0.588 over 516 subclasses with depressed levels. A translation test maps our predictor linearly into the original Eq. 13; because the map is affine inside the exponent, rank statistics are unchanged *by construction* (their bit-identical values, Spearman 0.6787 / Pearson 0.7976 in both variants, serve only as an implementation sanity check), so only levels are informative: top software domains published at 216%/yr reach only ~47%/yr under translation (~79%/yr under our refit). Errors-in-variables and quantile–quantile analyses (Sections 4.3–4.4) confirm that the high-end compression is not an artifact of the refitted slope or of OLS attenuation but resides in the measured X distribution's upper tail on our substrate (q99 − q50 span 0.346 vs. 0.454), and the tested linear crosswalk cannot remove it.
3. **A patent-wise normalization crosswalk.** On 2,974,440 patents matched between the two corpora, our own-centrality percentile correlates with the MIT own@t+3 percentile at Pearson 0.509 (Spearman 0.537), and our age-3 cited-percentile endpoint with MIT's cited@t−1 at 0.516 (0.541), with linear maps MIT_own_t3 = 0.4459·X_ours + 0.2214 and MIT_cited_t1 = 0.6722·X_ours + 0.0705 (residual SD 0.248 and 0.247). Crucially, MIT's *own internal* snapshot stability bounds what any crosswalk can achieve: own@t+3 vs. own@2015 correlate at only 0.448 within the MIT data itself (own@t+3 vs. cited@t−1: 0.752; our internal own-vs-cited: 0.612). The cited-side agreement rises monotonically by filing era — 0.296 (1980–84), 0.530 (1990–94), 0.664 (2000–04) — consistent with thinner historical citation coverage in the early world graph.
4. **A set-noise invariance result.** Greedy CPC signatures reconstruct the 30 gold-standard calibration patent sets only mediocrely (F1 median 0.496, range 0.084–0.778), yet the domain-mean predictor computed on signature sets correlates with the gold-set predictor at Pearson 0.987. The domain-average centrality is nearly invariant to set-boundary noise: what matters is hitting the right technological neighborhood, not the exact set membership. This quantitatively justifies pattern-based domain proxies in production.
5. **Treatment of retroactive CPC migration.** Living classification systems silently invalidate static domain definitions. In our semiconductor case study, DOCDB's retroactive H01L→H10x reclassification leaves a naive H01L-based definition matching only 852 patents in the benchmark join (8,978 classification rows remain under H01L, while H10* holds 1.34 million distinct patents); a curated pattern union (H01L plus H10B/D/F/H/K/N/P/W) restores the domain to 1,011,919 patents, yielding K = 12.4%/yr and a steady trajectory in place of an artificial collapse.
6. **A rolling K(t) extension with explicit reliability gates.** Beyond the static published rates, we compute rolling 5-year-window rate estimates and a direction classification (accelerating / steady / maturing / decelerating), guarded by density gates (minimum window occupancy, median window n ≥ 1000 for direction calls), re-centered classification bands, a truncation horizon that discards citation-incomplete years (last complete year: 2019), withholding of absolute rates above the calibrated range (CALIB_MAX = 50%/yr), and a tiered face-validity control group (designed as four tiers; the thin-domain withhold tier is currently unpopulated, Appendix A.1) that the cited-predictor configuration passes on 10 of 11 checks (partially in-sample, since the direction bands are re-centered on related mundane controls; Section 4.7, Appendix A.1). A preregistered, fully out-of-sample ten-domain direction holdout (expectations committed before evaluation) scores 4/10: the false-positive control holds (4/4 mundane domains, zero polarity errors), but no up/down expectation is met — the direction layer is validated as a non-false-alarm system, not as an acceleration detector (Section 4.7, Appendix A.2).

Section 3 describes our data substrate, implementation, and the limitations of the approach. Section 4 reports the recalibration, the domain-level benchmark, the patent-level crosswalk, the set-noise invariance and CPC-migration analyses, and the K(t) direction validation. Section 5 discusses the findings and outlines future work; Section 6 concludes.

## 2 Related work

### 2.1 Patent characteristics as correlates of improvement rates

Benson and Magee (2015) provided the first systematic evidence that patent data encode the rate of technological progress. For 28 technological domains with curated functional-performance time series, they operationalized concepts from the technical-change literature — effort, importance, recency, immediacy — as domain-level patent metrics and tested each against the empirically measured improvement rate *K*. Importance, recency, and immediacy of a domain's patents all correlated strongly with *K*; a combined importance–immediacy metric reached r = 0.76 (p = 2.6×10⁻⁶), was robust to domain selection, and retained predictive power more than ten years into the future. The study established the foundational hypothesis of the entire line: differences in improvement rates across domains can be accounted for by differences in the domains' patent characteristics.

### 2.2 Empirical measurement of the rates themselves

Magee, Basnet, Funk, and Benson (2016) supplied the ground-truth side. Analyzing performance trends and patent output over time for the same 28 domains, they showed that all 28 domains exhibit high-quality exponential fits of log performance against time (r² > 0.7), whereas power-law ("experience-curve") fits with patents as the effort variable fail badly in 9 domains (r² < 0.5). They verified Sahal's equation for additional effort variables (patents, revenue) and found that the fitted time exponent — the improvement rate *K* — is insensitive to the choice of effort variable, whereas the power-law exponents are not. Their interpretation, that time-based exponential (Moore-type) descriptions capture long-term, multi-design technological change better than experience curves, underpins the constant-*K* assumption of the subsequent prediction work; their fitted *K* values are the calibration targets used by all later studies (extended from 28 to 30 domains).

### 2.3 The normalized-centrality predictor

Triulzi, Alstott, and Magee (2020) turned correlation into a validated prediction method. For 30 domains they tested six patent-based predictors, emphasizing that raw citation measures are confounded by changing patenting dynamics and must be normalized: each patent's SPNP centrality (Hummon and Doreian, 1989; Batagelj, 2003), measured 3 years after grant, is compared against 1,000 randomized citation networks preserving in-/out-degree, citation-age profile, and main-USPC-class composition, converted to a z-score and then a rank percentile per grant year. Monte Carlo cross-validation (MCCV) over estimation years 1980–2013 (repeated 50/50 domain splits) yielded out-of-sample correlations of roughly 0.72 from the 1990s onward, with the normalized centrality of a domain's patents — in particular of the patents it cites — beating competing predictors including entropy and obsolescence indices. The authors explicitly caution that short-term TIRs are not reliably estimated, a caveat our K(t) gates (Section 3.7) take as a design requirement.

### 2.4 Predictions for all technologies

Singh, Triulzi, and Magee (2021) scaled the method to the full US patent system. Inverting the classification-overlap method, they intersected all UPC 3-digit × IPC 4-character class pairs (284,472 candidates), tested pair overlap against random expectation, assigned each patent to its largest-overlap pair, and retained pairs with ≥ 100 patents — yielding 1,757 disjoint technology domains containing 97.2% of the 5,083,263 US utility patents granted 1976–mid-2015. Applying the calibrated regression K̂ = exp(6.22·X − 4.97)·e^(σ²/2) to each domain's mean normalized centrality produced the first published improvement-rate predictions for essentially all definable technologies, ranging from 2%/yr (mechanical skin treatment) to 216%/yr (dynamic information exchange systems), with more than 80% of domains below 25%/yr and the fastest domains concentrated in software and algorithms. Leave-one-out analysis placed 63% of calibration predictions within ±10 and 87% within ±15 percentage points; own- and cited-centrality predictors correlate at 0.97 on the domain level. Rates were notably not a strong function of patent-set size. This 1,757-domain table (Zenodo 10.5281/zenodo.4779831) is the benchmark target of Section 4.2.

### 2.5 Commercial deployment and positioning

The method's practical relevance is evidenced by its commercialization: the first author of Singh et al. (2021) lists TechNext Inc. as an affiliation in the paper itself, and the SPNP-based improvement-rate methodology is protected in the United States by patent US 12,099,572 B2 assigned to that MIT spinout. To date, however, all published validations originate from a single research group, and the two prediction studies rest on a single corpus and a single normalization pipeline; the published output is a static per-domain rate. The present work provides, to our knowledge, the first independent replication on a structurally different corpus — a worldwide DOCDB citation graph with CPC classifications — together with a patent-level crosswalk quantifying exactly where the two measurement systems agree and diverge, and a gated dynamic extension beyond the static-*K* regime.

## 3 Data and methods

Our system implements the patent-based TIR prediction method developed at MIT (Benson and Magee, 2015; Magee et al., 2016; Triulzi et al., 2020; Singh et al., 2021) — referred to throughout as *the reference method* — re-built end-to-end on an independent, worldwide patent corpus, recalibrated against the original researchers' ground-truth data, and extended with a rolling-window estimator of the improvement rate over time. The pipeline comprises seven components: a citation graph built from the DOCDB world archive (Section 3.1), SPNP centrality computed in log space with a fixed-age forward cap (Section 3.2), a degree-, age-, and class-preserving randomization null model (Section 3.3), a cited-centrality predictor (Section 3.4), a log-linear calibration against 29 empirically measured domains (Section 3.5), CPC-pattern domain definitions (Section 3.6), and a rolling K(t) extension with explicit reliability gates (Section 3.7). Section 3.8 states the limitations of the approach. Table 1 summarizes the correspondence between the reference method and our implementation.

### 3.1 Patent corpus and citation graph

We build the citation network from the DOCDB worldwide bibliographic archive, covering all patent offices and kind codes with no language filter. Nodes are individual DOCDB publication records; no family-level aggregation is applied, so an invention published by several offices or in several kind-code documents contributes multiple nodes (the consequences for centrality and normalization are stated in Section 3.8, item 11). The resulting graph (snapshot of 2026-07-11) contains 42.58 million nodes and 145.5 million citation edges spanning publication years 1784–2026. This contrasts with the reference corpus of 5,083,263 US utility patents granted between 1976 and June 2015 (Singh et al., 2021); the consequences of the different normalization population are quantified in Sections 4.2 and 3.8.

Two pragmatic conventions are applied. First, the publication year of each document serves as a proxy for its grant year, since harmonized grant dates are not available across all offices in DOCDB. Second, citation edges between documents published in the same year are dropped to enforce a strictly backward-in-time directed acyclic graph (DAG), which the path-counting algorithm requires.

Coverage relative to the reference corpus is partial: within the MIT window (US grants 1976–2015), our graph matches 2.975 million of the 5.26 million US patents in the researchers' patent-level reference file, i.e. approximately 57%. Matching is performed on normalized US patent numbers against DOCDB US publication numbers. A full audit of the match loss decomposes all 5,259,906 MIT reference rows by cause: 2,974,440 (56.5%) match a grant-kind DOCDB publication in the graph; 2,285,450 (43.5%) have no corresponding node in the graph at all (*not_in_graph*); only 16 match a grant-kind publication in the wrong year, and none match exclusively a non-grant kind code. The loss is therefore almost entirely genuine DOCDB coverage gap, not a numbering-, kind-code-, or family-resolution artifact. The accompanying balance check reveals a systematic selection profile: matched patents average filing year 1992.2 with a mean of 15.6 citations, whereas unmatched (*not_in_graph*) patents average 2006.3 and 5.96 — the unmatched population is markedly younger and citation-poorer. The matched subset is thus biased toward older, better-cited patents; the consequences are discussed in Section 3.8, item 1. We further note, without resolving it here, that the patent-level reference file contains 5.26 million US patents whereas the published corpus size is 5,083,263 utility grants (1976–6/2015); we match against the file as released. All patent-level comparisons in Sections 4.1 and 4.3 are computed on this matched subset.

### 3.2 SPNP centrality

We use SPNP centrality on the citation network, following Hummon and Doreian (1989) and the algorithmic formulation of Batagelj (2003): SPNP(v) is the product of the number of ancestor paths into v and descendant paths out of v (each incremented by one), so that a patent scores highly when it lies on many knowledge-flow paths through the citation graph. Because path counts grow super-exponentially on a 42.58-million-node graph, our implementation works entirely in log space, accumulating layer-wise log-sum-exp terms (log n⁻ + log n⁺) rather than raw counts; this is numerically safe and otherwise identical to the counting definition.

The reference method measures SPNP three years after grant (t+3) to compare patents at a fixed maturity. We reproduce this as an *age-3 forward cap*: backward citations are always counted in full (a patent's reference list is complete at grant), while forward citations are restricted to edges whose citation age is at most 3 years. This reconstructs, for every patent simultaneously, the graph "as of grant + 3" and removes the immaturity bias that would otherwise favor older cohorts.

### 3.3 Null model normalization

Raw SPNP is confounded by degree, cohort age, and field-specific citation practices. Following Triulzi et al. (2020), each patent's observed log-SPNP is compared against its distribution over R randomized networks that preserve, per patent, (i) in- and out-degree, (ii) the citation-age profile, and (iii) the share of same-class citations. We implement the constrained randomization as a grouped permutation of destination endpoints within buckets defined by (source year, destination year, source class, same-class flag): permuting destinations inside such a bucket preserves the same constraint set as the reference method's constrained edge swap, keeps the strictly backward DAG, and is fully vectorized with no rejection sampling. We do not claim formal equivalence of the two null models: whether the reference implementation's MCMC edge-swap sampler targets the same distribution as our uniform within-bucket permutation is not established; destination permutation can generate duplicate source–destination pairs; and when the same-class flag is false, only the same-class share — not the exact destination-class composition per patent — is preserved. A formal statement of the preserved quantities and the target null distribution, together with the treatment of duplicate and self-referential citations, is given in Appendix C. Two parameters differ from the reference implementation: we use R = 100 randomizations (versus 1,000), and class preservation uses the 3-character CPC prefix (versus the main USPC class, which does not exist in DOCDB). The observed log-SPNP is converted to a z-score against the randomized mean and standard deviation, and z-scores are then converted to rank percentiles within each grant-year cohort, yielding the normalized centrality X ∈ [0, 1] used throughout; X̄ denotes its unweighted mean over a domain's patents.

The full SPNP build with R = 100 completes in 114 minutes on a single CPU core (i9-11900K, 64 GB RAM) at a constant ≈10–15 GB memory footprint and requires no GPU; R = 1,000 would take roughly 19–22 hours single-core for a noise reduction that scales only with √R. A direct R-ablation on a US subgraph shows that R = 100 is empirically sufficient at the levels the method consumes (Section 3.8, item 2).

### 3.4 Predictor

The reference method's best-performing predictor is not the centrality of a domain's own patents but the mean normalized centrality of the patents they *cite* (Triulzi et al., 2020; Singh et al., 2021, Eq. 13). We adopt this "cited" predictor: for each patent, the mean percentile of its cited patents; for each domain, the unweighted mean of this quantity over all domain patents. One approximation is made explicit: the reference implementation measures the cited patent's centrality one year before the citing patent (a t−1 snapshot), whereas we use the cited patent's age-3-capped end value. The age cap already provides a fixed-age normalization, which covers most of the temporal effect the snapshot is designed for; the residual discrepancy is quantified in Sections 3.8 and 4.3.

The choice is empirically grounded on our own substrate: calibrated against the exact MIT domain patent sets and ground-truth improvement rates (Section 3.5), the cited predictor outperforms the own predictor on all three criteria we evaluate (Section 4.1), reproducing the direction of the reference method's internal finding (on the MIT dataset itself, the cited construction achieves R² = 0.621 versus 0.549 for own centrality; at the domain level the two predictors correlate at 0.97 in the reference data). One implementation warning is worth recording: the cited predictor must be computed from the full citation graph. An earlier attempt using a link subset in which historical US grants retained only 0.1% of their backward citations inverted the predictor comparison; recomputation from the complete graph (same coverage as own centrality) restored it.

### 3.5 Calibration

Ground truth follows the MIT performance-curve tradition: for each empirically measured domain, the true improvement rate K_true is the OLS slope of ln(performance) on year from curated performance time series (Benson and Magee, 2015; Magee et al., 2016). We calibrate against the *exact* MIT domain memberships rather than approximations, using the researchers' patent-level dataset of 511,506 (domain, patent) rows across 30 calibration domains; one domain (magnetic materials) lacks a performance time series, leaving n = 29. MIT patent numbers are matched to our US-grant publication numbers, and the domain predictor X̄ is the mean cited-percentile over the matched set.

The calibration is a log-linear OLS, ln K = a + b·X̄, with a normal-theory retransformation exp(σ̂²/2) on the back-transformation — the parametric correction under a log-normal error assumption. (This is not Duan's nonparametric smearing estimator, whose factor (1/n)·Σ exp(êᵢ) can differ numerically at n = 29; the two factors should be compared once and the choice documented in the submitted version.) The active production formula is

  K̂ = exp(−5.5622 + 5.5036·X̄) · exp(0.4930/2),

with fit statistics reported in Section 4.1. The coefficients are deliberately substrate-bound: because the normalization population differs (worldwide DOCDB versus US-only PatentsView), our X̄ must never be inserted into the reference Eq. 5 (K̂ = exp(6.22·X − 4.97)·e^(σ²/2)), and vice versa.

*External benchmark.* As an out-of-calibration check, we compare our predictions with the 1,757 published domain forecasts (Zenodo record 4779831). Because the published data do not include patent membership lists, the COM domains are reconstructed from the public patent-level file (Mendeley doi:10.17632/f4fj887y67.1) via main-class × IPC4 pairs; reconstruction quality, the full benchmark results, and the US-only versus worldwide corpus effect are reported in Section 4.2.

### 3.6 Domain definitions

Production domains are defined as curated sets of CPC code patterns (subclass and main-group prefixes, assembled with embedding-assisted curation; the curation protocol — embedding model, retrieval and similarity criteria, manual accept/reject steps — is not specified in this draft and must be documented in an appendix of the submitted version), used as an explicit proxy for the reference method's COM sets, which cannot be reconstructed outside the US classification system. The adequacy of this proxy is tested directly in Section 4.5: even mediocre set reconstructions leave the domain-mean predictor nearly unchanged, which quantitatively justifies the pattern proxy — for TIR estimation, capturing the correct technological neighborhood matters far more than the exact set boundary.

A corpus-specific hazard is CPC migration: DOCDB applies CPC reorganizations *retroactively*, so a pattern query on a historical code sees only the residual population and produces an artificial collapse in recent years. The semiconductor class H01L is the measured example (Section 4.6). We therefore expand affected patterns with a curated migration map (H01L → H10B, H10D, H10F, H10H, H10K, H10N, H10P, H10W, derived from the CPC definition texts and holdings checks), which restores the domain and replaces an artifactual collapse with a plausible trajectory.

### 3.7 Rolling K(t) extension

The reference model assigns each domain a single, time-constant K under the Generalized Moore's Law assumption. Our implementation adds a temporal layer with no counterpart in the reference method: X̄ is computed in rolling 5-year windows over the domain's patents, each window is mapped through the calibration of Section 3.5, and the resulting K(t) curve is summarized by K_median (full history) and K_recent. A direction label (accelerating / steady / maturing / decelerating) is derived from the relative change of the recent fit. To fix the terminology precisely: K_recent is the median of the windowed K(t) values over the last complete 7-year span ending at the truncation horizon (last complete year 2019; TRUNC below), i.e., it lies immediately *before* the excluded citation-immature years, not inside them. The direction label is a banded classification of a relative-change statistic Δrel computed from the recent windowed fit; the band thresholds below are expressed in units of Δrel. A formula-level specification of Δrel (window indexing, fit form) and of the exact band logic accompanies the code release (see Data and code availability); the prose here fixes the gate semantics.

Because short-horizon readings are the least reliable part of the method (Section 3.8, item 9), the extension is wrapped in explicit honesty gates:

- **Window density (MIN_N).** A window with fewer than 100 patents is not reported.
- **Domain density (DOMAIN_MIN_TOTAL).** Domains with fewer than 500 distinct patents in total return "insufficient data".
- **Direction density gate.** The direction label is withheld ("uncertain") when the median patent count over the recent fit windows is below 1,000; measured on mundane control domains, thin windows (median n ≈ 450–790) inflate the relative change to 0.3–0.5 (false "accelerating"), while the same domains at high density collapse to ≈ 0.15.
- **Truncation (TRUNC).** The most recent 7 years carry immature forward citations and are excluded; the last complete year is 2019. (An age-cap-based relaxation to 2022 has been validated with re-centered bands but is not the reported default.)
- **Re-centered direction bands.** Direction thresholds are centered on an empirical neutral baseline — the median relative change over 12 dense, mundane mechanical domains (fasteners, milling, gears, etc.) — because the recent edge carries a universal drift that would otherwise be misread as a trend. For the cited predictor the neutral point is −0.158, giving bands of −0.008 / −0.348 / −0.608. Predictor and bands must switch together. These 12 tuning domains overlap with the mundane tier of the validation control group; Section 4.7 and Appendix A.1 disclose this overlap and its consequence (partially in-sample validation).
- **Calibration ceiling (CALIB_MAX).** Absolute K̂ values above 50%/yr lie outside the calibrated range (the training maximum K_true is ≈ 47.5%/yr) and are withheld; only the direction is shown.

Direction output is evaluated against two control sets: a tiered in-sample control group of technologies with known expected behavior and a preregistered, fully out-of-sample ten-domain holdout (Section 4.7, Appendix A).

### 3.8 Limitations of the approach

We state the limitations of the method explicitly, since several are structural rather than incidental.

1. **Corpus coverage.** The DOCDB graph matches only ≈ 57% (2.975 M of 5.26 M) of the reference US patents in the 1976–2015 window, and historical citation coverage is thinner in early years: the patent-level correlation of our cited predictor with MIT's rises from 0.296 for 1980–84 filings through 0.530 (1990–94) to 0.664 (2000–04). Early-cohort centralities are therefore noisier than recent ones. The match loss has now been audited (Section 3.1): of 5,259,906 MIT reference rows, 2,974,440 (56.5%) match, 2,285,450 (43.5%) are absent from the graph entirely, 16 match only in the wrong year, and none match only under a non-grant kind code — the loss is almost purely a DOCDB coverage gap. The balance check shows the loss is *not* random: unmatched patents are systematically younger (mean filing year 2006.3 vs. 1992.2) and citation-poorer (mean 5.96 vs. 15.6 citations), so the matched subset over-represents older, better-cited patents; patent-level comparisons on this subset (Sections 4.1, 4.3) carry that selection profile. Moreover, the worldwide normalization population is not the US-only population: on the world basis, agreement with the published forecasts drops from Spearman 0.704 to 0.588 and the median K̂ from 11.1%/yr to 6.6%/yr (Section 4.2). Worldwide scores are a scale of their own and must not be quoted as MIT-equivalent numbers.

2. **R = 100 randomizations.** We use one tenth of the reference method's R = 1,000, trading z-score precision for a 114-minute single-core build (versus an estimated 19–22 hours). The added per-patent noise largely averages out in domain means, and the benchmark of Section 4.2 shows no evident penalty *in the domain-level rank statistics*. A direct R-ablation now bounds the finite-R effect: on the US subgraph (6,742,889 nodes, 46.3 million edges), two independent R = 100 runs with distinct RNG seeds and one R = 300 run yield patent-level percentile correlations of Pearson 0.9911 (R100a↔R100b; top-decile Jaccard 0.873, mean |Δ| in the top decile 0.0099) and 0.9919 (R100↔R300; 0.919 / 0.0064), and domain-level X̄ identical across all three runs to within ±0.0005 (e.g., G06F 0.713/0.713/0.713; G06Q 0.7438/0.7440/0.7441; H04L, H04W, F16B, B65D, and E05B likewise). The finite-R hypothesis for the upper-tail compression of Section 4.4 is thereby refuted: R = 100 is empirically sufficient at both the percentile and domain-mean level, and an R = 1,000 rebuild (estimated 19–22 hours) is unnecessary. Individual patent percentiles remain marginally less stable than at higher R, with the residual improvement bounded by √R.

3. **Grant-year proxy and same-year edge drop.** Publication year stands in for grant year, so cohort assignment and the age-3 measurement point inherit whatever offset exists between publication and grant, which varies across offices and kind codes. Enforcing the citation DAG additionally discards same-year citation edges, removing a small amount of very-fast citation signal; the share and absolute number of dropped same-year edges have not been quantified.

4. **cited@age-3 approximation.** Our cited predictor uses the age-3-capped end value instead of the reference t−1 snapshot. The patent-level crosswalk of Section 4.3 quantifies the cost: cross-implementation agreement is moderate at the patent level (r ≈ 0.51–0.52) but must be read against the vintage variance *within* the MIT data itself (own@t+3 vs. own@2015: 0.448); at the level the method actually uses — domain means — agreement is high (Section 4.2).

5. **Single predictor.** The calibration is a one-variable OLS on a single citation-network statistic. Candidate predictors beyond SPNP-cited (entropy or obsolescence indices in the original work; composite centralities or text features elsewhere) are not used; any information they carry beyond cited-SPNP is discarded.

6. **Calibration ceiling and high-end compression.** The training ground truth tops out at ≈ 47.5%/yr, so the fit extrapolates flatly into the fast-software regime. Section 4.4 shows — confirmed by errors-in-variables regression and a quantile–quantile comparison — that the resulting high-end compression resides in the measured X distribution itself, not in the refit slope, and cannot be removed by the tested linear crosswalk. The CALIB_MAX = 50%/yr withhold (Section 3.7) exists precisely to prevent these compressed absolute values from being reported as calibrated.

7. **CPC migration beyond H01L.** The retroactive-reclassification artifact of Sections 3.6 and 4.6 is handled by a curated map for the one large case we detected (H01L → H10*). Other past or future CPC reorganizations may produce similar cliffs in other domains; there is no automatic detection, so undetected migrations would silently truncate a domain and bias its recent windows downward.

8. **Small ground-truth base.** All absolute calibration rests on 29 domains with performance time series. Our out-of-sample evidence is a leave-one-out R² of 0.516 with corr(K̂_LOO, K_true) = 0.721 (ln-Pearson; Spearman 0.722); unlike the reference work we report no Monte Carlo cross-validation. Uncertainty is now quantified by bootstrap (B = 10,000; Section 4.1): the calibration slope b carries a 95% CI of [4.05, 7.66] and the cited R² a CI of [0.318, 0.766] — wide, as expected at n = 29 — and the paired cited-versus-own comparison yields ΔR² CI [0.011, 0.102] with P(ΔR² > 0) = 0.9954, while the ΔSpearman CI touches zero ([−0.0015, 0.137], P = 0.9699). The 29-domain base bounds what any refit — ours or the reference method's — can claim about domains dissimilar to the calibration set.

9. **K(t) and direction lie outside the validated regime.** The reference method was built and validated for a single long-run K per domain, and its authors warn explicitly that short-term TIRs are not reliably estimated, arguing that short-run assessment is better served by direct engineering analysis. Our rolling 5-year K(t) and direction labels are exactly such short-horizon estimates. The density gates, truncation, and re-centered bands of Section 3.7 mitigate the known failure modes, and the tiered in-sample control achieves 10/11 — but the preregistered out-of-sample holdout scores only 4/10 (Section 4.7, Appendix A.2): the false-positive control holds fully (4/4 mundane domains, zero polarity errors), while none of the six up/down expectations is met. The direction layer is therefore validated as a *non-false-alarm system*, not as an acceleration or maturation detector; the failures are consistent with a construct mismatch between adoption/volume momentum (the basis of the external expectations) and the centrality trend the method actually measures (Appendix A.3). At the recent edge, single-domain direction remains noisier than the curve itself. K(t) and direction outputs are our own construct on MIT foundations and must not be presented as MIT-validated quantities — nor marketed as a detector of adoption or filing booms (see Section 5.5).

10. **Agreement with published forecasts is not agreement with the truth.** The strongest external check available to us (Section 4.2) is convergence with the 1,757 *published forecasts*, which are themselves model predictions; high agreement demonstrates faithful reproduction of the method, not correctness of the predicted rates. Section 5.6 develops this point.

11. **Publication-level graph (no family aggregation).** The graph is built on individual DOCDB publication records without family-level deduplication (Section 3.1). Equivalent publications of the same invention across offices and kind codes therefore enter as separate nodes, which can inflate degrees and path counts in an office-dependent way; this confounds both the centrality itself and the worldwide normalization population. The treatment of family-internal citations is likewise unaggregated, and the magnitude of these effects has not been quantified.

## 4 Results

All results in this section are computed on the production substrate: the worldwide DOCDB citation graph of Section 3.1 (42.58 million nodes, 145.5 million edges, publication years 1784–2026), with SPNP centrality measured under an age-3 forward cap, normalized against R = 100 degree-, age-, and class-preserving randomized networks, and expressed as within-grant-year rank percentiles. Within the 1976–2015 window of the original studies, this graph covers 2.975 million of the 5.26 million US patents in the MIT patent-level dataset (≈57%; see Section 3.1 for the match procedure and the completed loss audit).

### 4.1 Calibration on exact benchmark sets

We first calibrate our predictor against the exact benchmark used by the reference method: the released per-patent dataset covering the 30 calibration domains (511,506 (domain, patent) rows; 29 domains carry a performance time series and enter the regression), with K_true taken from the same curated performance series (Benson and Magee, 2015; Magee et al., 2016). For each domain, X̄ is the unweighted mean of our normalized centrality percentile over the domain's exact patent set; we fit ln K = a + b·X̄ with a normal-theory retransformation (Section 3.5). Table 2 reports in-sample fit, rank correlation, and leave-one-out (LOO) performance for the two candidate predictors: the domain patents' own centrality ("own") and the mean centrality of the patents they cite ("cited").

**Table 2.** Calibration against the exact benchmark patent sets (n = 29 domains). Bracketed intervals are 95% bootstrap CIs (domain-level resampling, B = 10,000).

| Predictor | R² [95% CI] | Spearman ρ [95% CI] | LOO-R² | corr(K̂_LOO, K_true), ln-Pearson / Spearman |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| own (domain patents' centrality) | 0.521 [0.253, 0.728] | 0.712 | 0.459 | 0.681 / 0.671 |
| **cited (centrality of cited patents)** | **0.574 [0.318, 0.766]** | **0.764 [0.534, 0.895]** | **0.516** | **0.721 / 0.722** |

The cited predictor outperforms the own predictor on all criteria, and the comparison is now quantified by a paired domain-level bootstrap (B = 10,000): ΔR² (cited − own) has a 95% CI of [0.011, 0.102] with P(ΔR² > 0) = 0.9954 — a clear advantage — whereas the ΔSpearman CI touches zero ([−0.0015, 0.137], P = 0.9699), so the rank-correlation advantage, while probable, is not individually resolved at the 95% level at n = 29. This reproduces on our substrate the internal finding of the reference work, where the cited predictor was likewise the winning candidate (Triulzi et al., 2020). The calibration slope is itself bootstrap-bounded at b ∈ [4.05, 7.66] (95% CI) — wide, as expected from 29 domains — and the resulting production calibration is

  K̂ = exp(−5.5622 + 5.5036·X̄) · exp(0.4930/2).

(The released bootstrap artifact reports the slope point estimate as 5.5037; the fourth-decimal discrepancy is rounding of the same fit, and the production constant 5.5036 is used consistently throughout this paper.)

For reference, recomputing the original construction on the original data (MIT predictor values against the same K_true) yields an in-sample R² of 0.621 for the cited predictor (consistent with the published correlation of r ≈ 0.80; Singh et al., 2021). Our 0.574 approaches this value despite a fully independent data basis: DOCDB instead of PatentsView, a worldwide rather than US-only graph, R = 100 instead of 1,000 randomizations, CPC-based class preservation in the null model, and an age-3 endpoint in place of the original temporal snapshots. Out of sample, our LOO-R² is 0.516. We deliberately do not convert this figure into a correlation for comparison with the reference method's MCCV statistic: √(LOO-R²) is not the correlation between LOO predictions and observed rates (LOO-R² = 1 − PRESS/SS carries no clean correlation interpretation), and the validation designs differ (LOO over 29 domains in ln-space here versus repeated 50/50 domain splits there). Computed directly, corr(K̂_LOO, K_true) is 0.721 (Pearson on ln K) and 0.722 (Spearman) for the cited predictor (own: 0.681 / 0.671). For context only: the reference method reports an average MCCV test correlation of ≈0.72 (Triulzi et al., 2020; Singh et al., 2021), and in its separate leave-one-out bootstrap over the calibration domains, 63% and 87% of predictions fell within ±10 and ±15 percentage points, respectively (Singh et al., 2021).

### 4.2 Domain-level benchmark against the 1,757 published predictions

We next benchmark against the full set of 1,757 published domain-level predictions (Singh et al., 2021), which cover 97.2% of the US patent system with predicted K ranging from 2%/yr to 216%/yr. Because the published data do not include patent membership lists, we reconstruct each domain from the released patent-level file via its defining class pair (USPC main class × IPC4). The reconstruction populates 1,751 of 1,757 domains with 2.82 million assigned patents; the log domain sizes correlate with the published sizes at Pearson r = 0.819, with a median size ratio of 0.724. The shortfall is expected: our reconstruction uses only the main-class pair per patent, whereas the original assignment used the full classification lists, so the reconstructed sets are systematic subsets of the published domains.

For each reconstructed domain we compute X̄ (mean cited-centrality percentile, US grants 1976–2015) and apply the production calibration of Section 4.1. Table 3 compares our K̂ with the published K̂ at two occupancy floors.

|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| n ≥ 100 | 1,299 | 0.675 [0.637, 0.709] | 0.772 [0.745, 0.796] | 10.0 | 10.97 |
| **n ≥ 500** | **651** | **0.679 [0.623, 0.726]** | **0.798 [0.762, 0.828]** | **11.0** | **11.02** |

At the n ≥ 500 floor, rank agreement is ρ = 0.679 (95% CI [0.623, 0.726]) with Pearson r = 0.798 (CI [0.762, 0.828]) on ln K; the level medians nearly coincide at this floor (11.0 vs. 11.02%/yr) and differ by about one percentage point at n ≥ 100 (10.0 vs. 10.97%/yr). One comparison we deliberately avoid: the reference method's out-of-sample correlation with *observed* rates under MCCV is ≈0.72, but that is a predicted-versus-observed statistic on held-out domains under a different validation design (and a Pearson-type figure, whereas our headline 0.679 is a Spearman) — it is not a ceiling for the agreement between two implementations of the same method. Two runs of one pipeline would correlate near 1.0, and two models of moderate accuracy can correlate with each other far above their accuracy. Read on its own terms, our 0.679 indicates substantial but incomplete cross-implementation agreement: corpus and implementation variance leave a measurable gap (Section 5.6 discusses what this benchmark can and cannot establish).

Two sensitivity checks support this reading. First, an earlier, coarser benchmark aggregating the 1,757 domains to 544 IPC4 subclasses (size-weighted geometric mean of K̂) and restricting our side to US-only grants yields ρ = 0.704 and Pearson r (ln K) = 0.774 over 526 matched subclasses, with medians of 11.1 (ours) versus 10.0%/yr (published) — consistent with Table 3 at lower granularity. Second, repeating that comparison with our production worldwide corpus definition instead of US-only degrades agreement to ρ = 0.588 / r = 0.662 over 516 matched subclasses and depresses the median to 6.6%/yr. This quantifies the corpus effect: normalizing against a worldwide patent population shifts both ranks and levels relative to the US-only benchmark. Production values computed on the world corpus therefore constitute their own scale and should not be read as reproductions of the published US-based numbers.

The essential reference frame is the fourth row: within the original dataset itself, the same patent's normalized own centrality measured at t+3 correlates with its 2015-endpoint measurement at only 0.448. Patent-level normalized centrality is thus not a stable patent property even within a single implementation — the choice of measurement point alone moves it more than our entire substrate change does. Against that yardstick, our patent-level agreement of 0.509 (own vs. own @ t+3) and 0.516 (cited vs. cited @ t−1) *exceeds* the reference method's own consistency between its two measurement points. The low 0.228 of our (endpoint-style) own measure against the MIT 2015 endpoint reflects the compounding of both effects (measurement-point drift plus substrate change) and confirms that the t+3 / age-3 alignment is the appropriate comparison.

Agreement is also strongly era-dependent. Restricting the cited-vs.-cited comparison by filing period gives Pearson r = 0.296 (1980–1984, n = 232,403), 0.530 (1990–1994, n = 505,819), and 0.664 (2000–2004, n = 438,494) — monotonically increasing, consistent with thinner historical backward-citation coverage in DOCDB for the earliest cohorts rather than a methodological divergence.

Finally, simple linear crosswalks between the scales are well determined but noisy at the patent level: MIT own @ t+3 = 0.4459·X_ours + 0.2214 (residual SD 0.248) and MIT cited @ t−1 = 0.6722·X_ours + 0.0705 (residual SD 0.247). Being OLS fits on noisy patent-level regressors, these slopes are attenuation-biased toward zero (errors-in-variables) — a property that matters for their use in the translation test of Section 4.4. Errors-in-variables regression quantifies the bias directly: on the cited pair, the patent-level OLS slope of 0.672 rises to 1.648 under Deming regression (λ = 1; n = 2,523,147) — strong attenuation at the patent level, as anticipated. At the level the method actually consumes, however, the two scales are nearly 1:1: over 645 domains with n ≥ 500 matched patents on both sides, the domain-mean X̄ pair correlates at Pearson 0.814 with an OLS slope of 0.914 and a Deming slope of 1.153, bracketing unity. The attenuation concern is therefore a patent-level phenomenon that averages out in domain means; its consequence for the tail-compression question is resolved in Section 4.4.

### 4.4 The high-end compression is a measurement property, not a recalibration artifact

Sections 4.2–4.3 leave one systematic discrepancy: our predictions compress the extreme upper tail. The fastest published domains — software and networking subclasses — carry published rates of 160–216%/yr, whereas our refit assigns them ~62–80%/yr. Two explanations are possible: (i) our refit slope (b = 5.5036, trained on K_true ≤ ~47.5%/yr) simply extrapolates more flatly than the original Eq. 5 slope (b = 6.22), i.e., a recalibration artifact; or (ii) the top tail of the X distribution itself differs on our substrate, i.e., a measurement property.

We discriminate between these with a translation test: instead of our refit, we map our patent-level X through the linear crosswalk of Section 4.3 onto the MIT cited scale and then apply the original Eq. 13 calibration. Because both maps are affine transformations inside the exponent, domain *ranks* are invariant **by construction**; accordingly, the n ≥ 500 benchmark statistics are bit-identical between the refit and the translated variant (ρ = 0.6787, Pearson r (ln K) = 0.7976 in both cases; medians 11.02 vs. 10.6%/yr against the published 11.0), which serves only as an implementation sanity check, not as a finding. Only the *levels* are informative, and they do not decompress (Table 5).

| Domain code | Published K̂ | Ours (refit) | Ours (translated via crosswalk + original Eq. 13) |
|---|---|---|---|
| 719/G06F | 216 | 79.5 | 46.9 |
| 709/G06F | 207 | 79.0 | 46.7 |
| 726/G06F | 195 | 75.7 | 45.2 |

Translated onto the original scale and pushed through the original calibration, the software top domains reach only ~39–47%/yr — further from the published 160–216%/yr than our own refit (~62–80%/yr). The obvious objection — that the crosswalk is an OLS fit on noisy patent-level data (residual SD ≈ 0.25 on a [0,1] percentile scale), whose attenuation-biased slope of 0.67 mechanically regresses tail values toward the center — has now been tested directly, and it does not rescue the tail. First, the errors-in-variables analysis of Section 4.3 shows that while patent-level attenuation is real (OLS 0.672 vs. Deming 1.648), the domain-level crosswalk, where item-level noise averages out, is nearly 1:1 (OLS 0.914, Deming 1.153 over 645 domains) — there is no hidden slope deficit at the level the calibration consumes. Second, a direct quantile–quantile comparison of the two implementations' domain-level X̄ distributions, which involves no regression at all, shows the compression explicitly: our quantiles at q50/q75/q90/q95/q99 are 0.5665 / 0.6509 / 0.7771 / 0.8513 / 0.9125 against MIT's 0.3996 / 0.4977 / 0.6411 / 0.7417 / 0.8536 — our distribution sits higher overall, but its upper-tail span q99 − q50 is 0.346 versus MIT's 0.454, i.e. genuinely about 24% shorter. One residual qualification remains: the test covers the fitted linear crosswalk, not all conceivable affine maps, though a single affine map matching the bulk of the distribution cannot simultaneously stretch the tail separately. The attenuation caveat is thereby resolved: the high-end compression is not an artifact of the refit slope or of OLS attenuation but a genuine measurement property of the X distribution on our substrate, and no linear crosswalk can restore it. This finding independently motivates the production policy of withholding absolute K̂ above CALIB_MAX = 50%/yr and reporting only ranks and directions in that regime (Section 3.7).

### 4.5 Robustness of the domain-mean predictor to set noise

Production domains are defined by curated CPC pattern sets, which are proxies for the exact benchmark sets (Section 3.6). To quantify the cost of this proxy, we constructed greedy CPC-maingroup signatures for the 30 gold-standard domains and compared them with the exact sets. As set reconstructions the signatures are mediocre: median F1 = 0.496, ranging from 0.084 (hybrid corn) to 0.778 (electrical telecommunication / electrical information transmission; MIT domain label ELECTRIC_TELECOM). Yet the predictor computed on the signature sets is almost indistinguishable from the predictor computed on the gold sets: X̄_gold vs. X̄_signature correlate at Pearson r = 0.987 across the 30 domains. (The greedy signature construction — candidate pool of CPC main groups, selection objective, and stopping rule — is specified, with pseudocode, in Appendix B.)

The domain-mean centrality predictor is thus nearly invariant to substantial set-boundary noise: patents erroneously included by a signature are drawn from the same technological neighborhood and carry similar normalized centralities, so the mean barely moves. What matters for the predictor is selecting the correct technology neighborhood, not the exact set boundary. This quantitatively justifies the CPC-pattern proxy used in production. The residual set effect is visible only at the calibration margin: replacing CPC-proxy sets with the exact benchmark sets raised the own-predictor calibration R² from 0.42–0.46 (the range across earlier proxy-set variants) to 0.521 (Section 4.1) — consistent with a small X̄ perturbation (1 − r ≈ 0.013) being amplified in a 29-point regression, while domain rankings remain essentially unaffected.

### 4.6 Case study: CPC reclassification and the semiconductor domain

Class-defined domains inherit the classification system's revisions, and DOCDB applies CPC reclassification retroactively. The semiconductor domain provides a stark case. With the CPC reorganization of the semiconductor classes (H01L → H10x, effective ~2023), the legacy prefix was progressively evacuated: in our per-year series, patents matching H01L fall from ~117,000 in 2019 to 88 in 2022 — a collapse that reflects no real-world phenomenon whatsoever. In the current CPC assignment table, an `H01L%` pattern retains only 8,978 rows, while the successor H10 hierarchy covers 1.34 million distinct patents; in the domain-level benchmark of Section 4.2, an H01L-defined semiconductor domain matched a mere 852 patents.

The curated pattern union of Section 3.6 (H01L plus H10B/H10D/H10F/H10H/H10K/H10N/H10P/H10W) restores the domain: membership rises from 852 to 1,011,919 patents, the estimated rate is K̂ = 12.4%/yr, and the direction label reads *steady* — replacing an artifactual late-period collapse with a plausible trajectory. The general lesson is that any longitudinal patent-class analysis must treat class-defined domains as versioned objects: retroactive reclassification can silently move a million patents out of a legacy prefix, and naive prefix matching then manufactures spurious decline. Benchmark comparisons against historical US-class-defined domains (Section 4.2) are affected in the same way and require migration mappings before any domain-specific claim.

### 4.7 Direction classification: in-sample control group and preregistered holdout

The rolling K(t) trajectory and its direction classification (Section 3.7) are extensions of our implementation with no counterpart in the reference static-K method, which explicitly cautions that short-term rates are not reliably estimated (Triulzi et al., 2020). They therefore cannot be benchmarked against published values. We evaluate them against two control sets: an eleven-domain tiered control group assembled during development (in-sample in part; Appendix A.1) and a ten-domain holdout whose expected directions were preregistered — fixed and committed before any evaluation — and which is disjoint from all tuning domains (Appendix A.2).

**In-sample control group (10/11).** The first set comprises rising fields that must read accelerating, maturing fields that should read maturing or decelerating, and mundane mechanical domains that must not read accelerating. Two disclosures qualify the counts. First, the mundane tier overlaps with the 12 mundane domains used to re-center the direction bands (Section 3.7; the overlap is domain-by-domain documented in Appendix A.1), and the selection of the cited predictor as the production configuration was itself partly informed by its elimination of the own predictor's single mundane false positive; band tuning and validation are therefore not disjoint, and the mundane counts — and with them the 10/11 total — are partially in-sample. Second, the expectations were author-assigned and not preregistered, so hindsight bias cannot be excluded; Appendix A.1 documents the full domain list, tier assignments, expectations, and per-domain results, including the wind-power expectation inconsistency with an earlier six-technology panel (which scored 6/6 under its own expectation scheme).

**Table 6.** In-sample direction control group: correct labels by tier (own predictor vs. production cited predictor with re-centered bands; full table in Appendix A.1).

| Tier (expectation) | own | cited |
|---|---|---|
| Rising → accelerating (3 domains) | 3/3 | 3/3 |
| Maturing → maturing/decelerating (2 domains) | 1/2 | 1/2 |
| Mundane → must not accelerate (6 domains) | 5/6 | 6/6 |
| **Total** | **9/11** | **10/11** |

On this partially in-sample control, the production cited predictor scores 10/11, improving on the own predictor (9/11) by eliminating the single mundane false positive (fasteners); the single miss is wind power, reading *steady* (Δrel = −0.047) where the tier expects maturing.

**Preregistered out-of-sample holdout (4/10).** To remove the in-sample qualification, a fresh ten-domain holdout — three expected-up domains (LiDAR, heat pumps, hydrogen), three expected-down (optical data storage, photographic film, ICE fuel injection), and four mundane must-not-accelerate controls (locks, writing instruments, plumbing, mechanical watch movements) — was assembled with per-domain expected directions and external justifications committed to version control four minutes *before* the evaluation ran (commit provenance in Appendix A.2). The set is disjoint from the band-tuning domains, the eleven-domain control, and the curated production subclasses. On the production configuration the holdout scores **4/10**: all four mundane controls pass (no false *accelerating*), and all six directional expectations fail — the up-tier domains read steady or uncertain, and the down-tier domains read steady or uncertain rather than maturing/decelerating (Table A.2).

The tier structure of this result is more informative than the total. The system commits **zero polarity errors**: no expected-up domain is labeled maturing/decelerating, no expected-down domain is labeled accelerating, and no mundane domain is labeled accelerating. Its *specificity* — the false-positive control that the abstention-first gate architecture of Section 3.7 was designed around — survives out-of-sample transfer intact (4/4, consistent with the 6/6 mundane tier in-sample). Its directional *sensitivity* does not: 0/6 on up/down calls. The direction layer is thus validated as a non-false-alarm system, not as a detector of acceleration or maturation.

**Post-hoc diagnostic and construct-mismatch interpretation.** A post-hoc re-examination of the directional misses under the relaxed truncation horizon (TRUNC = 4, last complete year ~2022; secondary analysis, not a rerun of the holdout; Appendix A.3) shows that recovering the 2020–2022 signal does not repair the misses: hydrogen stays *steady* (Δrel ≈ −0.0), LiDAR stays *steady* (−0.223), and ICE fuel injection flips to *accelerating* (+0.07) — against its expected direction. The pattern points to a construct mismatch rather than a tuning problem: the holdout expectations were justified by documented *volume and adoption* booms (filing counts, investment waves, national strategies), whereas the method measures a *centrality trend* — whether a domain's cited-SPNP percentile rises over recent windows. A field can boom in filings and adoption while its mean cited-centrality stays flat (many new entrants citing an established, non-rising body of prior art), and a declining field need not show a falling centrality trend. Adoption momentum and centrality-trend direction are distinct constructs, and the direction label must not be presented — or marketed — as a detector of the former (Sections 3.8 item 9 and 5.5). Absolute K̂ accompanies direction labels only within the calibrated range (≤50%/yr, Section 4.4); above it, only rank and direction are reported.

## 5 Discussion

### 5.1 Agreement across independent corpora and implementations: robustness as a finding

The central empirical result of this study is that an independent reimplementation of the SPNP-centrality method reproduces the published improvement-rate forecasts with substantial rank and level agreement. Our substrate shares neither data provenance nor code with the original: a worldwide DOCDB citation graph (versus 5.08 million US utility patents, 1976–2015, from PatentsView), covering only about 57% of the patents in the reference patent-level dataset within the 1976–2015 window. The implementation deviates deliberately in the randomization budget (R = 100 versus 1,000), the null-model class constraint (CPC 3-character prefixes versus main USPC class), the numerical formulation (log-space SPNP), the predictor construction (age-3-capped cited centrality versus a t−1 snapshot), and the calibration, which was refit on the exact original domain patent sets rather than adopting the published coefficients.

Despite this, the domain-level benchmark (Section 4.2) yields Spearman 0.679 and Pearson 0.798 (ln K) over the 651 domains with at least 500 matched patents, with nearly identical median rates at that floor (11.0 versus 11.02%/yr). We deliberately refrain from measuring this figure against the reference method's MCCV correlation of about 0.72: that statistic captures predictions against *observed* rates on held-out domains under a different design and correlation type, and it is no ceiling for cross-implementation agreement (Section 4.2 and Section 5.6). Read on its own terms, the agreement is substantial but incomplete — corpus and implementation variance leave a measurable gap.

The patent-level picture (Section 4.3) sharpens this interpretation. Individual normalized centralities agree only moderately across implementations (r ≈ 0.51), but the reference data are internally no more stable (own@t+3 versus own@2015: 0.448). Patent-level normalized centrality is a noisy quantity even within one implementation and one corpus; the forecasting signal is carried by domain-level aggregation, which is precisely where the two implementations converge. We regard this as an independent conceptual replication in the strict sense — same construct, different data and different operationalization. Its scope, however, must be stated precisely: domain *rankings* derived from the citation-network regularity prove robust to corpus and implementation variance, while absolute levels and the upper tail are substrate-bound (Sections 4.2 and 4.4), and the anchor to measured reality runs exclusively through the original 29-domain ground truth (Section 5.6).

### 5.2 The corpus effect: normalization populations do not travel

Triulzi et al. (2020) explicitly leave open "the comparison of TIRs estimates for the same domain using patent data from multiple national patent offices … to validate if the estimates are office-independent," adding that they believe they should be, but that "whether this is confirmed remains a question for future research." Our design permits a first quantitative answer, because the same substrate can be evaluated with US-restricted and worldwide patent populations (Section 4.2). At IPC4 granularity, the US-restricted variant reproduces the published forecasts with Spearman 0.704 and Pearson 0.774 (ln K) over 526 subclasses and matches levels (median 11.1 versus 10.0%/yr); the worldwide variant degrades to Spearman 0.588 and Pearson 0.662 over 516 subclasses and depresses levels substantially (median 6.6 versus 10.0%/yr).

The answer is therefore differentiated: rankings are approximately office-independent once populations are matched, but absolute levels are not, because rank-percentile normalization is defined relative to a population. Changing the population changes the measurement. This has a direct methodological consequence: calibration coefficients are substrate-bound and must never be transplanted — a predictor value from one corpus inserted into another corpus's regression produces a category error, not an estimate. Every reimplementation on a new corpus requires refitting against ground truth, as done here. The patent-level agreement gradient by filing era (0.296 → 0.530 → 0.664; Section 4.3) indicates that differential historical citation coverage between corpora is a principal driver: where the two graphs see similar citation environments, the normalized centralities converge.

### 5.3 Invariance to set noise: neighborhoods matter more than boundaries

Practically the most consequential finding concerns the sensitivity of the predictor to domain membership errors (Section 4.5). Membership noise that would be fatal for set-level retrieval — set-overlap F1 median 0.496 — is nearly irrelevant for the improvement-rate predictor (gold-vs.-signature predictor correlation 0.987), because the mean centrality of a technological neighborhood is highly homogeneous relative to between-domain differences. The same pattern appears in the contrast between patent-level (≈0.51) and domain-level (0.68–0.80) cross-implementation agreement: averaging over domains cancels item-level noise.

The consequence for scalable technology catalogs is significant. Exact, curated domain boundaries — the most labor-intensive component of the classification-overlap approach — are of secondary importance for rate estimation; what must be right is the technological neighborhood. Automated or semi-automated signature generation over standard classification systems can therefore support catalogs far larger than 1,757 domains without degrading the predictor, provided the retrieved sets remain centered on the intended technology.

One caveat is essential: the invariance is demonstrated for *neighborhood-preserving* membership noise — signature errors drawn from the same technological vicinity, which is exactly the mechanism Section 4.5 identifies — not for arbitrary random misassignment (for which it has not been tested and would plausibly fail) and not for systematic set truncation. Retroactive classification migration is the salient failure mode of the latter kind, as the semiconductor case study shows (Section 4.6): a naive legacy-prefix domain collapses to 852 matched patents and manufactures an artificial break, while the curated pattern union restores a million-patent domain with a steady trajectory. Boundary curation can be light-touch, but classification-system dynamics must be monitored.

### 5.4 Compression at the top of the rate distribution

Our implementation systematically compresses the fastest domains. Software and networking subclasses that the published forecasts place at 95–121%/yr (up to 216%/yr for the fastest domain, 719/G06F) emerge from our refit at roughly 26–41%/yr at IPC4 level (G06F 114→40, H04L 95→32, G06Q 121→41, H04W 101→26%/yr) and at ≈79.5%/yr for 719/G06F at domain level. The translation test of Section 4.4 isolates the cause: rankings are unchanged under a crosswalk into the original calibration (by construction), while translated levels for the top domains fall even further (to ≈47%/yr). The errors-in-variables and quantile–quantile analyses of Sections 4.3–4.4 settle what the translation test alone could not: the domain-level crosswalk slope is ≈1:1 (OLS 0.914, Deming 1.153), so no attenuation artifact hides in the fit, while the QQ comparison shows our domain-level X̄ upper-tail span (q99 − q50 = 0.346) is genuinely about 24% shorter than MIT's (0.454). The compression is an established measurement property of the predictor distribution on our substrate, and the tested linear crosswalk cannot repair it.

Of the candidate mechanisms, one is now adjudicated: finite-R sampling noise (R = 100 versus 1,000) is *refuted* as a cause by the R-ablation of Section 3.8, item 2 — percentiles are stable across independent R = 100 runs and against R = 300 (Pearson ≥ 0.991, domain means within ±0.0005), so a larger randomization budget would not lengthen the tail. The remaining candidates — residual influence of the worldwide normalization population even on US-restricted domain means, and thinner historical citation coverage — remain hypotheses. We note that tail difficulty is not unique to our implementation: the original leave-one-out analysis reports its largest discrepancies precisely at the fast end (+55 pp for Electric Computation, −21 pp for Optical Telecommunications, the fastest empirically measured domain at 65%/yr; Singh et al., 2021).

The honest operational consequence, implemented in the production system, is to withhold absolute rate estimates above the calibration limit: the training ground truth extends only to ≈47.5%/yr, and a hard gate at 50%/yr suppresses absolute values beyond it, reporting only ranks and direction. The alternative — tail-specific or nonlinear calibration — is a research task (Section 5.7), not a patch.

### 5.5 Time-varying K(t): a gated extension beyond the validated zone

The reference method deliberately estimates a single, time-constant K per domain, and Triulzi et al. (2020) warn explicitly that "short term TIRs are not reliably estimated," arguing that short-run assessment is better served by direct engineering evaluation. Our production system nevertheless computes a rolling K(t) over 5-year windows with a direction classification, because the practical value is real: the phase of a domain — accelerating, maturing, decelerating — carries strategic information about adoption timing that a static rate cannot, in the spirit of the performance-crossover framework linking improvement rates to S-curve dynamics (Benson and Magee, 2018). The tension between this utility and the published warning is not resolvable by assertion; we address it with the abstention-first gate architecture of Section 3.7 — density gates, truncation against citation immaturity, re-centered direction bands, and the calibration ceiling — as a middle path.

Validation now rests on two control sets with sharply different messages (Section 4.7, Appendix A). The partially in-sample tiered control group is classified 10/11 correctly with the cited predictor, including 6/6 on the must-not-accelerate mundane tier — which overlaps with the band-tuning domains. The preregistered out-of-sample holdout, disjoint from all tuning domains, scores 4/10: the false-positive control transfers perfectly (4/4 mundane, zero polarity errors across all ten domains), but none of the six directional (up/down) expectations is met, with the post-hoc diagnostic pointing to a construct mismatch between adoption/volume momentum and the centrality trend the method measures (Appendix A.3). The honest characterization is therefore asymmetric: the direction layer is a *validated non-false-alarm system* — when it says accelerating, that call has survived every specificity test we ran on the production configuration (the non-production TRUNC = 4 diagnostic of Appendix A.3 produced one wrong-direction *accelerating*, on ICE fuel injection, and is excluded by design) — but it is *not* a validated acceleration or maturation detector, and it must not be marketed as a detector of adoption or filing booms. K(t) and its direction remain outside the validated zone of Triulzi et al. (2020) and Singh et al. (2021), and we present them accordingly: as gated, ordinal signals with explicit abstention, not as short-term TIR point estimates.

### 5.6 Limitations of the benchmark logic

Our headline benchmark compares forecast against forecast, not forecast against realized improvement. Agreement with the published estimates demonstrates robustness of the method to corpus and implementation variance; it does not demonstrate accuracy, and the two implementations could share blind spots. Both are calibrated to the same small ground truth — 29–30 domains with curated performance time series (Benson and Magee, 2015; Magee et al., 2016) — and both inherit its composition, the same log-linear functional form, and the same reliance on citation-network structure. Errors are therefore plausibly correlated; the original authors' own diagnosis that the Electric Computation patent set is imperfect (Singh et al., 2021) would, for example, propagate into any implementation trained on it. The comparison statistics are also not equivalent to the reference validation: the MCCV correlation of ≈0.72 measures predicted against observed rates on held-out domains, whereas our 0.679 measures two forecasts on a reconstructed domain assignment (and compares a Spearman figure with a Pearson-type one); neither figure bounds the other. Reconstruction error (main-class pairs only; median size ratio 0.724) deflates observable agreement, while shared method structure inflates it relative to a true accuracy measure — the net bias is unknown. Finally, the absolute anchor remains n = 29 domains; our LOO-R² of 0.516 is the honest bound on out-of-sample explanatory power at that anchor.

### 5.7 Future work

The evidence collected here defines a concrete agenda:

1. ~~**Canonical randomization budget (R = 1,000).**~~ — **completed:** the R-ablation on the US subgraph (two independent R = 100 runs plus R = 300; Section 3.8, item 2) shows percentile correlations ≥ 0.991 and domain means stable to ±0.0005, refuting finite-R noise as a tail-compression mechanism and establishing R = 100 as empirically sufficient; the R = 1,000 rebuild (~19–22 h) is unnecessary.
2. **Faithful t−1 cited predictor via per-year snapshots.** Our age-3-capped endpoint approximates the reference t−1 temporal construction; a faithful reconstruction requires per-year SPNP snapshots (~2 hours of randomization per snapshot year). The expected gain is bounded: our construction reaches R² = 0.574 against ground truth, versus 0.621 for the temporal construction evaluated on the reference dataset's own predictor columns.
3. **Fixed-date snapshot comparisons.** These are largely substitutable by the reference dataset's own column pairs — own@t+3 versus own@2015 correlate at only 0.448 within the original data, bounding what a dedicated 2015-snapshot rebuild could add; the per-year snapshots of item 2 would cover the remainder.
4. **A COM-native domain table in the production system.** The reconstructed main-class × IPC4 assignment (1,751/1,757 domains, 2.82 million patents, ln-size correlation 0.819) should be promoted from benchmark artifact to a first-class technology-domain catalog, giving persistent one-to-one comparability with the published forecasts.
5. **Tail-specific calibration.** Nonlinear, segmented, or quantile-based fits above the current calibration limit, so that the high end can eventually be estimated rather than withheld.
6. **Extending the ground truth beyond 29 domains.** New curated performance time series, following the methodology of Magee et al. (2016) and Benson and Magee (2015); the original authors themselves note that prediction quality will improve as more historical performance data become available (Singh et al., 2021).
7. **Historical-event validation of K(t) direction.** Testing direction labels against documented historical accelerations and maturations, beyond the current control sets (the 11-domain in-sample group, the six-technology panel, and the 4/10 preregistered holdout of Appendix A), to move the dynamic extension toward the validated zone — with expectations grounded in centrality-relevant events rather than adoption volume, per the construct-mismatch finding.
8. **Science and funding signals as leading-indicator tiers.** Scientific publications and funding events precede patenting; integrating them as upstream tiers could extend the forecast horizon that citation maturation currently truncates at the last complete year (2019).
9. ~~**Uncertainty quantification.**~~ — **completed:** bootstrap CIs (B = 10,000) are now reported for the calibration, LOO, and benchmark statistics (Tables 2 and 3), including the paired cited-versus-own comparison (ΔR² CI [0.011, 0.102], P = 0.9954; ΔSpearman CI touching zero); Sections 4.1–4.2 and 3.8, item 8.
10. ~~**Errors-in-variables crosswalk and tail analysis.**~~ — **completed:** Deming regression (patent-level 1.648 vs. OLS 0.672; domain-level 1.153 vs. 0.914) and the QQ comparison (tail span 0.346 vs. 0.454) establish the tail compression as a genuine measurement property, not OLS attenuation (Sections 4.3–4.4).
11. ~~**Preregistered direction control set.**~~ — **completed:** the ten-domain holdout with expectations committed before evaluation scores 4/10 — specificity validated (4/4 mundane, zero polarity errors), directional sensitivity not (0/6); Section 4.7 and Appendix A.2.
12. ~~**Matching audit.**~~ — **completed:** the ≈43% match loss is almost entirely genuine DOCDB coverage gap (2,285,450 of 5,259,906 rows not in graph; only 16 wrong-year and 0 kind-code cases), with unmatched patents systematically younger (2006.3 vs. 1992.2) and citation-poorer (5.96 vs. 15.6); Section 3.1.
13. **Null-model sampler comparison.** Recomputing z-scores under a rejection-based simple-graph edge swap on a subgraph and correlating them with the bucket-permutation z-scores, to test the second-order conjecture of Appendix C.4 — the sampler-difference counterpart of the now-completed R-count ablation (item 1).
14. **Combined momentum signal.** The construct mismatch identified by the holdout (Section 4.7, Appendix A.3) suggests composing the centrality-trend direction with an orthogonal volume/takeoff trend from the leading-indicator tiers (item 8) into a combined momentum signal, so that adoption booms and centrality shifts are detected by the constructs that actually measure them.

## 6 Conclusion

We asked whether centrality-based improvement-rate estimation survives transplantation to an independent corpus and an independent implementation. It does: on a worldwide citation graph of 42.58 million patents, with a tenfold smaller randomization budget — shown by ablation to be empirically sufficient — a different classification system in the null model, a different predictor timing, and an independent recalibration, our forecasts reproduce the 1,757 published domain estimates at Spearman 0.679 (95% CI [0.623, 0.726]) and Pearson 0.798 (ln K; CI [0.762, 0.828]) over 651 well-populated domains, with matching medians (11.0 versus 11.02%/yr) — substantial but incomplete agreement, with a measurable gap left by corpus and implementation variance (and, as argued in Sections 4.2 and 5.6, no valid yardstick in the reference method's predicted-versus-observed MCCV statistic). The replication is informative about the method's structure: rankings are robust and approximately office-independent, absolute levels are bound to the normalization population and require per-substrate recalibration, the domain-mean predictor tolerates substantial neighborhood-preserving membership noise (enabling scalable catalogs) but not systematic classification migration, and the top tail of the rate distribution is compressed — demonstrably as a measurement property of the substrate, not a regression artifact — in ways the tested linear crosswalk cannot repair, and is therefore withheld above the calibration limit in production. Time-varying extensions remain outside the validated zone and are deployed only behind abstention gates; a preregistered direction holdout validates their false-positive control (4/10 overall, 4/4 mundane, zero polarity errors) but not their directional sensitivity. That the entire reproduction runs on a single CPU core underscores the practical accessibility of the method. Its limits should be stated as precisely as its successes: domain rankings prove robust to corpus and implementation variance, while absolute levels and the upper tail remain substrate-bound, and the anchor to measured reality remains confined to 29 domains with the original ground truth.

**Reading.** On the clean out-of-sample holdout the production path scores **4/10**. All four passes are in the mundane *not_up* tier (which only requires the absence of a false *accelerating*), and all six failures are in the directional (up/down) tiers: no rising domain was labeled accelerating and no maturing domain was labeled maturing/decelerating. This is a materially weaker result than the partially in-sample 10/11 of A.1 and is consistent with the honest characterization in Sections 3.7, 3.8 (item 9) and 4.7 that the direction extension operates outside the validated static-K regime and that its recent-edge discrimination is the least reliable part of the method. The mundane tier's robustness carries over; the directional tiers do not.

### A.3 Post-hoc diagnostic under relaxed truncation (secondary analysis)

The following is a **post-hoc, secondary analysis** — not part of the preregistration and not a production configuration. It re-examines a subset of the up/down holdout failures under the age-cap-based relaxed truncation horizon (TRUNC = 4, last complete year ~2022; Section 3.7), which recovers the 2020–2022 signal and which, per the code, requires its own re-centered bands. It is reported only to characterize where the misses come from and must not be read as a rerun of the holdout or as validation.

Under TRUNC = 4:

- **Hydrogen (C01B3, up):** label unchanged *steady*, Δrel ≈ −0.0 (rounds to zero from the −0.075 at TRUNC = 7) — still no acceleration signal despite the documented 2020s investment wave.
- **ICE fuel injection (F02M, down):** label *accelerating*, Δrel ≈ 0.07 — **against the expected maturing direction**, i.e. the relaxed horizon moves this domain the wrong way relative to its external expectation.
- **LiDAR (G01S17, up):** label *steady*, Δrel ≈ −0.223 — still no acceleration despite the documented autonomous-driving boom.
- **Heat pumps (F25B30), optical storage (G11B7), photographic film (G03C):** unchanged from the preregistered run (heat pumps *uncertain*; optical storage and film *uncertain*), i.e. the density/maturity gates continue to withhold or the direction does not move.

**Interpretation (construct mismatch).** These diagnostics point to a construct mismatch rather than a tuning problem. The holdout expectations were justified by documented *volume and adoption* booms — filing counts, investment waves, national strategies — whereas the method measures a *centrality trend*: whether a domain's cited-SPNP percentile is rising over recent windows. A field can experience a large filing/adoption boom while its mean cited-centrality stays flat or drifts down (many new entrants citing an already-established, non-rising body of prior art), and conversely a declining field need not show a falling centrality trend. LiDAR and hydrogen exemplify the former: large, real activity booms that do not translate into a rising centrality signal. The relaxed-horizon numbers do not repair this; in the case of fuel injection they invert the sign. The general implication, consistent with Section 3.8 (item 9), is that adoption/volume momentum and centrality-trend direction are distinct constructs, and the direction label should not be marketed as a detector of the former.

## Appendix B — Greedy CPC signature construction (pseudocode)

This appendix specifies the greedy CPC-signature procedure whose results are reported in Section 4.5 (median F1 = 0.496 across 30 gold domains, range 0.084–0.778; X̄_gold vs. X̄_signature Pearson r = 0.987). The procedure builds, for each MIT gold-standard domain, a signature = a set of CPC main groups whose union approximates the domain's exact patent membership, then evaluates that signature exactly against the gold list. The retrieval universe is US grants 1976–2015 in the fullz3 substrate (utility grants matching `US-<digits>-(A|B1|B2)`).

```
INPUT
  GOLD[d]        : set of gold patent numbers for domain d (MIT 511k list)
  UNIVERSE       : US utility grants 1976–2015 (fullz3), each with its CPC main groups
  maingroup(p)   : set of CPC main groups of patent p   (CPC code up to "/", e.g. "H01L21")

PRECOMPUTE
  G[d]        = |{ p in UNIVERSE : p in GOLD[d] }|               # matchable gold size
  gold_cnt[d][g] = # gold patents of d carrying main group g     # SQL GROUP BY
  glob[g]     = # UNIVERSE patents carrying main group g          # global group size

FOR EACH domain d:
  # --- candidate filter: a group must cover a non-trivial slice of the gold set ---
  CAND = { g : gold_cnt[d][g] >= max(5, 0.005 * G[d]) }
  order CAND by descending precision proxy  gold_cnt[d][g] / glob[g]   # purest first

  # --- greedy add by APPROXIMATE F1, union approximated as a SUM ---
  chosen = [] ; hit_apx = 0 ; ret_apx = 0
  repeat:
    f1_now = 2*hit_apx / (ret_apx + G[d])                # current approx F1 (recall denom = G)
    best = none
    for g in CAND \ chosen:
        # union approximated additively: assume the new group's gold and universe
        # counts are disjoint from what is already chosen (no overlap subtraction)
        f1_new = 2*(hit_apx + gold_cnt[d][g]) / ((ret_apx + glob[g]) + G[d])
        if f1_new > f1_now and (best is none or f1_new > best.f1):
            best = (f1_new, g, gold_cnt[d][g], glob[g])
    if best exists:
        chosen.append(best.g)
        hit_apx += best.gold_cnt ; ret_apx += best.glob      # additive union proxy
    else:
        break
  if chosen is empty:
    chosen = [ argmax_g gold_cnt[d][g] ]                    # fallback: single best group

  # --- EXACT evaluation of the final signature (one SQL pass, true set union) ---
  SIG   = DISTINCT { p in UNIVERSE : maingroup(p) ∩ chosen ≠ ∅ }   # true de-duplicated union
  ret   = |SIG|
  hit   = |{ p in SIG : p in GOLD[d] }|
  P     = hit / ret ;  R = hit / G[d] ;  F1 = 2PR / (P+R)
  # predictor perturbation, exact on both sets:
  X_sig  = mean cited_pctl over SIG
  X_gold = mean cited_pctl over { p in UNIVERSE : p in GOLD[d] }
  record (P, R, F1, X_gold, X_sig)

AGGREGATE over domains:
  f1_median, f1_min, f1_max
  pearson( X_gold[·], X_sig[·] )        # the 0.987 set-noise-invariance statistic
```

**Design choices and limits.** (1) *Additive union approximation in the greedy loop.* The selection objective adds each candidate's gold-hit and universe counts as if groups were disjoint, ignoring patents carrying several chosen main groups; this overstates both the retrieved and hit counts during selection but makes each step O(|CAND|) with no per-step SQL, so the whole signature is chosen from three precomputed count tables. Because CPC main groups within a technology overlap, the approximation is optimistic and the greedy F1 is not the signature's true F1 — it is used only to *order* additions. (2) *Exact final evaluation.* The reported precision, recall, F1, and both X̄ values are computed on the true de-duplicated union of the chosen groups via a single SQL pass, so no approximation enters the numbers in Section 4.5. (3) *Scope limits.* Signatures are restricted to CPC main groups (no subgroup precision, no Boolean exclusions), the candidate filter `max(5, 0.5% of gold)` discards rare-but-specific groups, and the retrieval universe is US 1976–2015 only — the signatures are deliberately mediocre set reconstructions whose purpose is to stress-test the predictor's invariance to boundary noise, not to serve as production domain definitions.

## Appendix C — Null-model sampling scheme

This appendix formalizes the sense in which the bucketed destination-permutation used to normalize SPNP (Section 3.3) preserves the same constraint set as the reference method's edge-swap randomization, and states honestly where the two schemes differ. Consistent with the Section 3.3 review requirement, **no equivalence of the two schemes' target distributions is claimed.** The argument has three parts: (a) statistics both schemes preserve exactly, (b) respects in which they can differ, and (c) a conjecture — labeled as such — that the differences are second-order for the z-score the normalization actually consumes.

### C.1 Setup

The citation graph is a strictly-backward DAG: each directed edge $e = (s, d)$ runs from a citing patent $s$ to an older cited patent $d$, with $\mathrm{year}(s) > \mathrm{year}(d)$. Each node carries a grant year and a main class (3-character CPC prefix in our implementation; main USPC class in the reference). For each edge define the tuple

$$b(e) = \big(\mathrm{year}(s),\ \mathrm{year}(d),\ \mathrm{class}(s),\ \mathbb{1}[\mathrm{class}(s)=\mathrm{class}(d)]\big),$$

the *bucket* of Section 3.3 (source year, destination year, source class, same-class flag). Our randomization (implementation `_randomized_zscore`) holds all source endpoints fixed and, independently within each bucket, applies a uniformly random permutation to the destination endpoints of the edges in that bucket. The reference method (Triulzi et al., 2020, §5.3, following Alstott et al., 2017) performs constrained citation edge swaps: it swaps citations while respecting the age of citing and cited patents, and swaps within same-main-class citing–cited pairs when the pair is same-class and within any-cited-class pairs sharing the citing main class otherwise.

### C.2 Statistics preserved exactly by both schemes

Let a "constraint-preserving" rewiring be one that alters only which older patent each citation points to, subject to fixing bucket membership. Both schemes are of this form and therefore hold the following invariant on every realization:

1. **Out-degree of every node.** Each citing patent $s$ keeps exactly its original set of outgoing edges (only their destinations are relabeled), so out-degree is fixed node-by-node. In our scheme this is immediate because source endpoints are never touched; in the edge-swap scheme it is a swap invariant.

2. **In-degree of every node — in distribution, per bucket.** A within-bucket permutation of destinations is a bijection of the multiset of destination endpoints onto itself, so the *total* number of citations received within each bucket is fixed, and the marginal in-degree distribution per bucket is preserved. (Exact node-by-node in-degree is *not* fixed on a single realization by either scheme — that is what randomizes — but the degree sequence's bucket-level totals are invariant.)

3. **Citation-age profile.** Because $\mathrm{year}(s)$ and $\mathrm{year}(d)$ are both part of $b(e)$ and permutation acts only within a bucket, every rewired edge keeps identical citing- and cited-years; the age gap $\mathrm{year}(s)-\mathrm{year}(d)$ of each edge is invariant, and the strictly-backward DAG is preserved with no rejection step. The reference scheme likewise "respects the age of the citing and the cited patent."

4. **Same-class share per citing patent.** The same-class flag is part of the bucket, so a same-class edge can only be reassigned to another same-class destination and a cross-class edge to another cross-class destination (of the same source year and class). The share of a patent's citations going to its own class versus other classes is therefore preserved, matching the reference constraint "the share of citations going to the same and any other main class."

These are exactly the quantities the reference method reports preserving (in/out citation counts, the citing–cited time difference, and the same/other-class share). In this sense the two schemes hold the *same constraint set*.

### C.3 Where the schemes can differ

Preserving the same constraints does not make the two schemes sample the same distribution over the constrained space. They can differ in at least three ways:

1. **Sampling distribution over the constraint space.** Our scheme draws, independently in each bucket, a *uniformly random* permutation of that bucket's destinations — an exact i.i.d. draw from the uniform distribution over within-bucket rewirings, with no autocorrelation between draws. The reference edge-swap is a Markov-chain (MCMC) random walk on the space of constrained graphs; its stationary distribution and, critically, its mixing time are not established here, so the effective sampling distribution of a finite swap run is unknown and need not coincide with the uniform product measure our scheme targets. We do not claim the reference chain's stationary law is the within-bucket-uniform law.

2. **Duplicate (multi-)edges.** Destination permutation can map two edges of the same source-year/class bucket onto the same cited patent, producing duplicate source–destination pairs (parallel citations). Our scheme permits these multi-edges; a swap scheme that rejects moves creating duplicates would instead sample from the space of simple graphs. This is a genuine difference in the support of the two null distributions, not just in sampling weight.

3. **Same-class composition and self-references.** When the same-class flag is false, our bucket fixes only the *share* of cross-class citations of a given source class, not the exact destination-class composition per patent; the reference wording ("any other cited class") is compatible with this, but the two need not induce identical cross-class destination distributions. Separately, within-bucket permutation does not by construction exclude a patent being reassigned to cite itself or a same-year peer; self-loops and same-year edges are removed only by the strictly-backward DAG filter (which drops $\mathrm{year}(s)=\mathrm{year}(d)$ edges before randomization, Section 3.8 item 3), so self-references are handled by pre-filtering rather than by a swap-time constraint.

### C.4 Why the difference is plausibly second-order for the z-score (conjecture)

*The following is a conjecture, not a proof.* The normalization consumes, per patent $i$, only the **mean and variance** of the log-SPNP statistic over the $R$ randomized controls, combining them into the z-score $z_i = (\log\mathrm{SPNP}^{\mathrm{obs}}_i - \hat\mu_i)/\hat\sigma_i$ (and, downstream, its within-cohort rank percentile). It does not use higher moments, joint dependence across patents, or any full-distribution feature. Two samplers that preserve the same constraints (C.2) fix the *support-defining* quantities that dominate a patent's centrality — its in/out counts, the age profile, and the same-class share — so the first two moments of $\log\mathrm{SPNP}_i$ under either null are driven mainly by these shared constraints. The ways the schemes differ (C.3) affect predominantly the *higher-order shape and tail* of the per-patent control distribution and the *between-patent dependence*, to which $\hat\mu_i$ and $\hat\sigma_i$ are relatively insensitive; the rank-percentile step further compresses residual scale differences within each cohort. We therefore expect the choice of sampler to shift individual z-scores only modestly and domain means (unweighted averages over many patents, Section 3.4) less still. This is consistent with, though not proven by, the domain-level benchmark agreement of Section 4.2; the reference method itself notes that the extreme z-scores grow over time as an artifact of the randomization's expanding swap space (corrected there, as here, by the within-cohort rank-percentile transform), which is a reminder that the *tails* of the two nulls are the least comparable part and the mean/variance the most. A direct check — recomputing z-scores under a rejection-based simple-graph swap on a subgraph and correlating with the bucket-permutation z-scores — is left to future work (Section 5.7, item 13). The related R-count ablation has since been completed (Section 3.8, item 2), establishing stability of the bucket-permutation scheme across randomization budgets; the sampler comparison itself remains open, and until it is run the second-order claim remains a conjecture.
